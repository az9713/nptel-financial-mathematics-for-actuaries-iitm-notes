The force of interest, constant and variable

A saver will hold 100,000 dollars for five years and has four offers, all advertised with the number 8%. Bank A pays an effective annual rate of 8%, bank B quotes a nominal rate of discount of 8% convertible quarterly, bank C quotes a constant force of interest of 8% a year, and bank D quotes a force of interest that rises in a straight line from 6% a year now to 10% a year in five years. The offers are invented for this chapter. After five years the deposit is worth 146,932.81 dollars at A, 149,788.50 at B, and 149,182.47 at both C and D: the same advertised number spans 2,855.70 dollars. Offers C and D agree at year 5, but they do not agree on a second deposit. Fifty thousand dollars added at year 2 is worth 63,562.46 dollars at year 5 under C and 65,106.41 under D; the error the lecture warns against, integrating over the wrong three years, gives 62,055.12, which is 3,051.29 dollars short. This chapter defines the force of interest as the limit of nominal rates, derives its relations to the effective and nominal rates, lets it vary with time, and derives the accumulation function from it, so that these four offers and these two deposits can be compared on one scale.

The running case and the tools from earlier in the course L8 0:19

Earlier weeks of the course measured growth with an effective rate of interest, an effective rate of discount, and nominal rates of interest and of discount that are converted \(m\) times a year. All of these rates describe growth over a step of time: a year, or an \(m\)th of a year. This chapter asks what remains when the step shrinks to zero, and what changes when the rate itself changes with time. This section restates the tools the chapter uses. Each is taken as given from earlier in the course; none is derived again here.

Amount and accumulation functions. Time \(t\) is measured in years from the date of the first deposit, \(t\ge0\). The amount function \(A(t)\) is the value at time \(t\) of an investment, and the accumulation function \(a(t)\) is the value at time \(t\) of an investment of 1 made at time 0, so \(a(0)=1\). For an investment of \(A(0)\gt0\),

\[ A(t)=A(0)\,a(t),\qquad a(0)=1. \tag{3.1} \]

The letter \(a\) followed by a time in parentheses always denotes the accumulation function in this chapter. Under compound interest at the effective annual rate \(i\), \(a(t)=(1+i)^t\) for every real \(t\ge0\), not only for whole years. The discount factor is \(v=1/(1+i)\), and the effective annual rate of discount is \(d=i/(1+i)=1-v\), interest paid in advance as a fraction of the amount at the end of the year. All rates in this chapter satisfy \(i\gt-1\), so that \(1+i\gt0\) and its logarithm exists; most examples have \(i\gt0\).

Nominal rates. A nominal annual rate of interest convertible \(m\) times a year, \(i^{(m)}\), credits interest at the rate \(i^{(m)}/m\) for each \(m\)th of a year; the superscript \((m)\) is a label, not a power. A nominal annual rate of discount convertible \(m\) times a year, \(d^{(m)}\), charges discount at the rate \(d^{(m)}/m\) in advance for each \(m\)th of a year. Both are equivalent to the effective rate \(i\) when they produce the same growth over one year:

\[ \Bigl(1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^m=1+i,\qquad\text{so}\qquad i^{(m)}=m\bigl[(1+i)^{1/m}-1\bigr], \tag{3.2} \] \[ \Bigl(1-\frac{d^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^{-m}=1+i,\qquad\text{so}\qquad d^{(m)}=m\bigl[1-v^{1/m}\bigr]. \tag{3.3} \]

With \(m=1\) the nominal rates are the effective ones: \(i^{(1)}=i\) and \(d^{(1)}=d\).

Rounding. Every computation in this chapter carries its intermediate values unrounded. Amounts are rounded to cents, and rates and accumulation factors are printed to six decimals or as percentages to four decimals, only where they are displayed; a few quantities are printed with fewer decimals (years to four, percentage points to two, one day's interest to three). A displayed product of a printed factor and an amount, or a difference of two printed amounts, can therefore differ from the printed result in the last cent. The spread of the four offers, 2,855.70 dollars, is the difference of the unrounded values 149,788.50498 and 146,932.80768; the two printed amounts differ by 2,855.69. Likewise bank B's margin over C is 606.04 from unrounded values and 606.03 from the printed amounts. The program at the end of the chapter reproduces every amount of the running case from unrounded values.

The running case. A saver deposits 100,000 dollars at \(t=0\) and withdraws at \(t=5\). Four banks quote "8%" (all four quotes are invented for this chapter):

  • bank A: an effective annual rate of interest \(i=8\%\);
  • bank B: a nominal annual rate of discount convertible quarterly, \(d^{(4)}=8\%\);
  • bank C: a constant force of interest of 8% a year;
  • bank D: a force of interest that rises linearly from 6% a year at \(t=0\) to 10% a year at \(t=5\).

Three questions run through the chapter. Which bank gives the most at \(t=5\)? If the saver adds 50,000 dollars at \(t=2\), what is that deposit worth at \(t=5\) under C and under D? And what single deposit at \(t=0\) grows to 200,000 dollars at \(t=5\) under D? Banks A and B can be compared with the tools above. Banks C and D need a new object, the force of interest, which the next sections define. The formal model of the chapter is an accumulation function \(a(t)\) on a time axis, together with the rate of growth that generates it; every result below is a statement about one of these two objects or about the passage between them.

From nominal rates to the force of interest L8 4:36

Question. Interest can be credited once a year, monthly, daily or hourly. The lecture's question is what happens when the crediting interval shrinks without limit, so that \(m\to\infty\). Two different questions hide in this one, and they have different answers.

A fixed nominal rate. The slide opens with the statement that money grows faster as the frequency \(m\) increases. That statement holds when the nominal rate is held fixed. Let \(q\gt0\) be a fixed nominal annual rate (the letter \(q\) is used only in this paragraph) and compare the growth over one year, \((1+q/m)^m\), for different \(m\). Treat \(m\) as a real variable \(x\gt0\) and write \(f(x)=x\ln(1+q/x)\). With \(u=q/x\gt0\), the derivative is

\[ f'(x)=\ln(1+u)-\frac{u}{1+u}\gt0, \]

because \(\ln(1+u)=\int_0^u\frac{ds}{1+s}\) and the integrand is larger than \(1/(1+u)\) for \(0\le s\lt u\). So the growth \(e^{f(m)}\) increases with \(m\). At \(q=10\%\) it is 1.100000 for \(m=1\), 1.104713 for \(m=12\), and it tends to \(e^{0.1}=1.105171\). The calculus result the slide recalls is Euler's limit \(\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+1/n)^n=e\) and its generalization \(\lim_{m\to\infty}(1+q/m)^m=e^q\) for a fixed number \(q\).

A fixed effective rate. The equivalence (3.2) asks the opposite question: the growth over the year is fixed at \(1+i\), and the nominal rate \(i^{(m)}\) that produces it is computed for each \(m\). Then the growth does not increase at all, and \(i^{(m)}\) changes with \(m\). At \(i=10\%\), \(i^{(1)}=0.100000\), \(i^{(12)}=0.095690\) and \(i^{(365)}=0.095323\) (Table 3.1, in the section on where the force sits among the other rates). The force of interest is defined from this second question.

Definition. The force of interest equivalent to the effective annual rate \(i\) is the limit of the equivalent nominal rates,

\[ \delta=\lim_{m\to\infty}i^{(m)}. \tag{3.4} \]

The slide writes the limit as \(i^{(\infty)}\), denotes it by \(\delta\), and calls it the nominal annual rate of interest compounded continuously. Its unit is "per year", the unit of every nominal annual rate; the section on the force at an instant gives the unit a meaning at each instant.

Derivation of the value. The definition is useful only if the limit exists. Write \(L=\ln(1+i)\), so \((1+i)^{1/m}=e^{L/m}\) and, by (3.2),

\[ i^{(m)}=m\bigl(e^{L/m}-1\bigr)=L\cdot\frac{e^{u}-1}{u}\quad\text{with }u=\frac{L}{m}\ (L\neq0). \]

As \(m\to\infty\), \(u\to0\), and \((e^u-1)/u\to1\), because this quotient is the difference quotient of \(e^u\) at 0 and the derivative of \(e^u\) at 0 is 1. Hence \(i^{(m)}\to L\): the limit (3.4) exists and \(\delta=\ln(1+i)\). If \(L=0\), then \(i=0\) and every \(i^{(m)}\) is 0. The same computation with (3.3) gives \(d^{(m)}=m(1-e^{-L/m})=L\,(1-e^{-u})/u\to L\): the nominal rates of discount have the same limit.

The relation \(1+i=e^\delta\). Exponentiating \(\delta=\ln(1+i)\) gives

\[ 1+i=e^{\delta},\qquad \delta=\ln(1+i),\qquad i=e^{\delta}-1. \tag{3.5} \]

The slide reaches (3.5) by another route: it starts from \(1+i=(1+i^{(m)}/m)^m\), which holds for every \(m\), replaces \(i^{(m)}\) by its limit \(\delta\) inside the bracket, and applies the generalized Euler limit. That step needs more than the generalized limit, because \(i^{(m)}\) is not a fixed number: it changes with \(m\). The following lemma supplies the missing step.

Lemma. If a sequence \(x_m\) tends to a number \(x\), then

\[ \lim_{m\to\infty}\Bigl(1+\frac{x_m}{m}\Bigr)^m=e^{x}. \tag{3.6} \]

Proof. For large \(m\), \(|x_m/m|\lt1\), so the logarithm of the bracket exists. Let \(\ell(u)=\ln(1+u)/u\) for \(u\neq0\) and \(\ell(0)=1\); \(\ell\) is continuous at 0 because the derivative of \(\ln(1+u)\) at 0 is 1. Then \(m\ln(1+x_m/m)=x_m\,\ell(x_m/m)\to x\cdot1\), and the exponential function is continuous, so the power tends to \(e^x\). With \(x_m=i^{(m)}\) and \(x=\delta\), the lemma turns the slide's route into a proof: the left side \(1+i\) does not depend on \(m\), and the right side tends to \(e^\delta\). ∎

Interpretation. The equivalent nominal rates \(i^{(m)}\) describe the same growth over a year, credited more and more often. As crediting becomes continuous, the nominal rate needed for that growth falls to \(\delta\). Relation (3.5) is the exchange rate between the two descriptions: \(e^\delta\) is the growth of 1 over a year under continuous crediting at nominal rate \(\delta\), and it equals the growth \(1+i\) under one annual credit.

Boundary. The definition (3.4) gives one number \(\delta\) for one effective rate \(i\) that holds in every year. If the rate of growth changes during the year or from year to year, (3.4) does not apply; the sections on the variable force replace it with a definition at each instant.

Converting between i and the force: steps and a ramp L8 11:14

The lecture compares two pictures of growth over two years (slide 5 of Lecture 8). Under annual crediting, the balance stays at 1 until the end of the first year, jumps by the year's interest, stays at the new level for the second year, and jumps again: a staircase. Under continuous crediting, the balance rises without jumps: a ramp. The slide draws the staircase for an effective rate of 10% and the ramp \(e^{0.1t}\) for a force of 0.10, and the lecture points out that the two are not equivalent: at \(t=1\) the staircase reaches 1.10 and the ramp 1.105171. The slide's condition for equivalence is that both reach the same height \(1+i\) at \(t=1\).

Figure 3.1 adds the curve that is equivalent to the staircase. Its force follows from (3.5): \(\delta=\ln1.10=0.095310\), the 9.53% of the slide. Between year ends, the staircase and the equivalent curve are different models. The staircase is \(a(t)=(1+i)^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\), where \(\lfloor t\rfloor\) is the largest whole number not above \(t\); it credits nothing until a year is complete. The compound model \(a(t)=(1+i)^t\) of (3.1) grows within the year: at \(t=1.5\) it gives 1.153690 against the staircase's 1.10. The two models agree at whole years, which is all that an effective annual rate specifies.

Conversion in both directions. By (3.5), an effective rate converts to a force by \(\delta=\ln(1+i)\), and a force converts to an effective rate by \(i=e^\delta-1\). The slide's trap alert is that \(\delta\) is always slightly less than \(i\): a quoted effective 10% must not be used as a force of 10%. The size of "slightly" depends on \(i\): at 10% the force is 0.47 percentage points lower, and the series of the next section gives the gap as about \(i^2/2\) for small \(i\). The claim "always" holds for every \(i\neq0\) with \(i\gt-1\); the next section proves it.

Banks A and C. Bank A's effective 8% corresponds to the force \(\ln1.08=0.076961\). Bank C's force of 8% corresponds to the effective rate \(e^{0.08}-1=8.3287\%\). Bank C therefore pays more than bank A, although both advertise 8%; the next section computes by how much.

Accumulation and discounting under a constant force L8 17:17

Accumulation function. If the force \(\delta\) is constant over time, the accumulation function of compound interest becomes, by (3.5),

\[ a(t)=(1+i)^t=\bigl(e^{\delta}\bigr)^t=e^{\delta t},\qquad \mathrm{FV}=\mathrm{PV}\cdot e^{\delta t},\qquad \mathrm{PV}=\mathrm{FV}\cdot e^{-\delta t}. \tag{3.7} \]

Here \(\mathrm{PV}\) is an amount at time 0 and \(\mathrm{FV}\) is its value at time \(t\), the labels of the slide. The discounting formula divides the accumulation formula by \(e^{\delta t}\); the discount factor for one year is \(v=e^{-\delta}\). The exponent \(\delta t\) is a pure number: \(\delta\) is per year and \(t\) is in years. If time were measured in months, the force per month would be \(\delta/12\).

Checks. The lecture notes that \(\delta=0\) gives \(e^\delta=1\), so \(1+i=1\) and \(i=0\): no growth. For \(\delta\gt0\), \(e^{\delta t}\) is increasing and exceeds 1 for every \(t\gt0\). At whole years (3.7) reproduces compound interest at \(i=e^\delta-1\), by construction.

The lecture's doubling example. An investment grows at a constant force of 5% a year. How long does it take to double? The condition \(e^{0.05t}=2\) gives, after taking logarithms, \(0.05t=\ln2\) and

\[ t=\frac{\ln 2}{0.05}=\frac{0.693147}{0.05}=13.8629\text{ years}, \]

the slide's 13.86 years. At an effective rate of 5% the doubling time is \(\ln2/\ln1.05=14.2067\) years, 0.3438 years longer, because a force of 5% corresponds to the effective rate \(e^{0.05}-1=5.1271\%\).

The running case, constant-force offers. Under bank A, the deposit grows to \(100{,}000\times1.08^5=146{,}932.81\) dollars. Under bank C, by (3.7), it grows to \(100{,}000\,e^{0.08\times5}=100{,}000\,e^{0.4}=149{,}182.47\) dollars, 2,249.66 dollars more. The difference is the gap between a force of 8% and a force of 7.6961%, compounded over five years.

Boundary. Formula (3.7) assumes one force for the whole term. A force that changes at a known date, or continuously, needs the integral formula derived in the section on the accumulation function.

Where the force sits among the other rates L8 22:03

The series. The lecture expands \(\delta=\ln(1+i)\) in its Maclaurin series (the Taylor series around 0),

\[ \delta=\ln(1+i)=i-\frac{i^2}{2}+\frac{i^3}{3}-\dots\qquad(-1\lt i\le1), \]

and keeps two terms, \(\delta\approx i-i^2/2\). The slide reads the truncated series as confirmation that \(\delta\lt i\). The full series does prove it on \(-1\lt i\le1\). For \(0\lt i\le1\) the terms \(i^n/n\) alternate in sign and decrease strictly to 0, so the sum lies strictly between any two consecutive partial sums: \(i-i^2/2\lt\delta\lt i\). For \(-1\lt i\lt0\) every term after the first is negative, so \(\delta\lt i\) again. The two-term truncation by itself is a size estimate, not the proof: at \(i=10\%\) it gives 0.095000 against the exact 0.095310, an error of 0.000310, close to the next term \(i^3/3=0.000333\). The series says nothing for \(i\gt1\), where it diverges; the next paragraph proves \(\delta\lt i\) for every \(i\gt-1\), \(i\ne0\), without a series.

Proof that \(\delta\lt i\). For every real \(y\neq0\), \(e^y\gt1+y\). Indeed, \(g(y)=e^y-1-y\) has \(g(0)=0\) and \(g'(y)=e^y-1\), which is negative for \(y\lt0\) and positive for \(y\gt0\), so \(g\) has its only minimum, 0, at \(y=0\). Apply this with \(y=\delta=\ln(1+i)\), which is non-zero when \(i\neq0\): \(1+i=e^\delta\gt1+\delta\), so \(\delta\lt i\). The proof uses neither \(i\gt0\) nor a series, so it covers negative rates as well, as the lecture states: at \(i=-5\%\), \(\delta=\ln0.95=-0.051293\), which is less than \(-0.05\).

The ordering of the five rates. The slide's "grand inequality" places the force among the four discrete rates: for any \(m\gt1\),

\[ d\lt d^{(m)}\lt\delta\lt i^{(m)}\lt i\qquad(i\gt0,\ m=2,3,\dots). \tag{3.8} \]

The derivation uses two integral representations. For \(z\neq0\),

\[ \frac{e^{z}-1}{z}=\int_0^1e^{zs}\,ds,\qquad \frac{1-e^{-z}}{z}=\int_0^1e^{-zs}\,ds, \tag{3.9} \]

which follow by evaluating the integrals. Apply them with \(z=\delta/m\gt0\) (positive because \(i\gt0\)). By (3.2), (3.3) and \(1+i=e^\delta\), \(i^{(m)}=m(e^{\delta/m}-1)\) and \(d^{(m)}=m(1-e^{-\delta/m})\), so

\[ i^{(m)}=\delta\int_0^1e^{\delta s/m}\,ds,\qquad d^{(m)}=\delta\int_0^1e^{-\delta s/m}\,ds . \]

Three facts follow. First, for \(0\lt s\le1\), \(e^{\delta s/m}\gt1\gt e^{-\delta s/m}\), so the first integral exceeds 1 and the second is less than 1: \(d^{(m)}\lt\delta\lt i^{(m)}\). Second, as \(m\) increases, each \(e^{\delta s/m}\) decreases and each \(e^{-\delta s/m}\) increases, so \(i^{(m)}\) decreases in \(m\) and \(d^{(m)}\) increases in \(m\). Comparing with \(m=1\), where \(i^{(1)}=i\) and \(d^{(1)}=d\), gives \(i^{(m)}\lt i\) and \(d^{(m)}\gt d\) for \(m\ge2\). Third, both integrands tend to 1 as \(m\to\infty\), which recovers \(\lim i^{(m)}=\lim d^{(m)}=\delta\) of (3.4). Together these give (3.8), and they say more than (3.8): the interest rates fall towards \(\delta\) and the discount rates rise towards it as conversion becomes more frequent. The chain holds for \(-1\lt i\lt0\) as well: with \(z\lt0\) the integrands satisfy \(e^{\delta s/m}\lt1\lt e^{-\delta s/m}\), and multiplying by the negative \(\delta\) reverses each inequality, so the same three facts follow (at \(i=-5\%\) and \(m=2\): \(-0.052632\lt-0.051957\lt-0.051293\lt-0.050641\lt-0.05\)). At \(i=0\) all five rates are 0.

The "perfect center". The slide draws (3.8) on a number line, with \(\delta\) between the discount rates on the left and the interest rates on the right, and calls \(\delta\) the perfect center; the lecture also calls it a balance point. These are figures of speech. The precise statement is (3.8) with its two monotone sequences: \(\delta\) is strictly between \(d^{(m)}\) and \(i^{(m)}\) for every \(m\), and it is their common limit. It is not the midpoint of \(d\) and \(i\): at \(i=10\%\), the midpoint is \((0.100000+0.090909)/2=0.095455\), and \(\delta=0.095310\) is 0.000144 below it. The series \(d=i-i^2+i^3-\dots\) shows why the two are close: \((i+d)/2=i-i^2/2+i^3/2-\dots\) agrees with \(\delta\) in the first two terms and differs by about \(i^3/6\) (0.000167 at 10%).

The lecture's conversion from a discount rate. Given \(d^{(4)}=8\%\), find the equivalent force. The slide's strategy is to convert to \(1+i\) first: by (3.3) and (3.5),

\[ \Bigl(1-\frac{0.08}{4}\Bigr)^{-4}=1+i=e^{\delta},\qquad 0.98^{-4}=e^\delta,\qquad \delta=-4\ln0.98=0.080811, \]

the slide's 8.08%. The same numbers illustrate (3.8): \(1+i=0.98^{-4}=1.084166\), so \(i=0.084166\); \(d=1-0.98^4=0.077632\); and \(i^{(4)}=4(0.98^{-1}-1)=0.081633\) by (3.2). The chain is \(0.077632\lt0.08\lt0.080811\lt0.081633\lt0.084166\). The lecture calls this conversion skill of the utmost importance for Exam FM; that is the speaker's judgment. The published syllabus of the Society of Actuaries Exam FM lists the force of interest among the terms a candidate must define, and conversion among \(i\), \(i^{(m)}\), \(d\), \(d^{(m)}\) and the force of interest among its learning outcomes (SOA Exam FM syllabus, October 2026, p. 2).

Bank B. Bank B's quote is the lecture's example, so its force is 0.080811 and the deposit grows to \(100{,}000\times0.98^{-20}=100{,}000\,e^{5\times0.080811}=149{,}788.50\) dollars. The constant-force offers now rank B, C, A, in the order of their forces: 0.080811, 0.080000, 0.076961. Ordering the offers by force is equivalent to ordering them by effective rate, because \(i=e^\delta-1\) is increasing in \(\delta\); the advertised number 8% orders nothing, because it measures three different quantities.

The force at an instant L9 3:38

So far one number \(\delta\) has described the growth in every year. Bank D's force is not one number: it is 6% a year at \(t=0\) and 10% a year at \(t=5\). The lecture motivates a varying force with the observation that rates fluctuate and central banks change them; that is the speaker's illustration of why the model is needed, and the book makes no claim about any market from it. The question is mathematical: if the rate of growth can be different at every instant, what is "the rate at time \(t\)", and how does the accumulation function follow from it?

Derivation. The lecture builds the definition from the nominal rate of a short interval, as (3.4) was built from nominal rates over a year. Over the interval from \(t\) to \(t+1/m\), an amount \(A(t)\) earns \(A(t+1/m)-A(t)\). As a rate per \(m\)th of a year this is \([A(t+1/m)-A(t)]/A(t)\), and as a nominal annual rate it is \(m\) times that:

\[ i^{(m)}_t=m\cdot\frac{A(t+\frac1m)-A(t)}{A(t)} . \]

The slide works it first for quarters (\(m=4\)) and then for general \(m\). Substitute \(h=1/m\), so \(m\to\infty\) means \(h\to0\), and take \(1/A(t)\) out of the limit, since it does not depend on \(h\):

\[ \lim_{m\to\infty}i^{(m)}_t=\frac{1}{A(t)}\lim_{h\to0}\frac{A(t+h)-A(t)}{h}=\frac{A'(t)}{A(t)} . \]

The last step is the definition of the derivative. It assumes that \(A\) is differentiable at \(t\) and that \(A(t)\gt0\).

Definition. The force of interest at time \(t\) is

\[ \delta_t=\frac{A'(t)}{A(t)}=\frac{d}{dt}\ln A(t)=\frac{a'(t)}{a(t)}. \tag{3.10} \]

The middle form is the chain rule. The last form holds because \(A(t)=A(0)a(t)\) by (3.1): the constant \(A(0)\) cancels, so the force does not depend on the size of the investment. Slide 11 of Lecture 8 states (3.10) and calls it the relative rate of change of the accumulated value at time \(t\); Lecture 9 derives it as above.

Meaning and units. The force is a rate per year at an instant; nothing is paid at an instant. By the definition of the derivative, the interest earned over a short interval is

\[ A(t+h)-A(t)=A(t)\,\delta_t\,h+\text{(terms smaller than }h\text{ as }h\to0), \]

so \(\delta_t\,h\) is, approximately, the interest per unit invested over the next \(h\) years. At a constant force of 8%, a balance of 100,000 dollars earns \(100{,}000\times0.08/365=21.92\) dollars over one day by this approximation, and \(100{,}000(e^{0.08/365}-1)=21.92\) dollars exactly by (3.7) (21.918 and 21.920 before rounding). The product \(\delta_t h\) is a pure number only when \(h\) is in the unit of \(\delta_t\), here years.

Checks against the constant case and against simple interest. Question. Given the force \(\delta_t\) for \(t\ge0\), find the accumulation function. By (3.10), \(a\) must satisfy the differential equation

\[ a'(t)=\delta_t\,a(t),\qquad a(0)=1. \tag{3.11} \]

Assume in this section that \(\delta_t\) is a continuous function of \(t\); the section on step functions relaxes this.

Derivation. The lecture integrates the middle form of (3.10) from 0 to \(t\), with \(r\) as the variable of integration (in this chapter \(r\) is only an integration variable):

\[ \int_0^t\delta_r\,dr=\int_0^t\frac{d}{dr}\ln a(r)\,dr=\ln a(t)-\ln a(0)=\ln a(t), \]

since \(a(0)=1\) and \(\ln1=0\). Exponentiating gives the slide's master equation,

\[ a(t)=\exp\Bigl(\int_0^t\delta_r\,dr\Bigr). \tag{3.12} \]

Uniqueness. The derivation assumed that a positive, differentiable solution exists and then found it. Write \(\Delta(t)=\int_0^t\delta_r\,dr\); by the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(\Delta'(t)=\delta_t\). The function found is a solution: \((e^{\Delta(t)})'=\Delta'(t)\,e^{\Delta(t)}=\delta_t\,e^{\Delta(t)}\) by the chain rule, and \(e^{\Delta(0)}=e^0=1\). It remains to show that (3.11) has no other solution. Let \(b\) be any differentiable function with \(b'(t)=\delta_tb(t)\) and \(b(0)=1\). Then

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(b(t)e^{-\Delta(t)}\Bigr)=b'(t)e^{-\Delta(t)}-b(t)\,\delta_t\,e^{-\Delta(t)}=0, \]

so \(b(t)e^{-\Delta(t)}\) is constant and equal to its value 1 at \(t=0\): \(b(t)=e^{\Delta(t)}\), which is (3.12). The solution is unique, and it is positive for every \(t\), whatever the sign of \(\delta_t\). A negative force makes \(a\) fall but never reach 0.

Checks. With a constant force, \(\int_0^t\delta\,dr=\delta t\), and (3.12) gives \(e^{\delta t}\), which is (3.7). With the simple-interest force \(\delta_r=i/(1+ir)\) of the previous section, \(\int_0^ti/(1+ir)\,dr=\ln(1+it)\), and (3.12) gives back \(a(t)=1+it\). The program at the end of the chapter checks both numerically.

Moving money between two dates. An amount invested at \(t_1\) and held to \(t_2\ge t_1\) grows by the ratio of the accumulation function at the two dates. By (3.1) and (3.12),

\[ A(t_2)=A(t_1)\,\frac{a(t_2)}{a(t_1)}=A(t_1)\exp\Bigl(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta_r\,dr\Bigr),\qquad \mathrm{AV}=\mathrm{PV}\cdot\exp\Bigl(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta_r\,dr\Bigr), \tag{3.13} \]

where the second form uses the slide's labels: \(\mathrm{PV}\) is the amount at \(t_1\) and \(\mathrm{AV}\) its accumulated value at \(t_2\). The exponent subtracts \(\int_0^{t_1}\) from \(\int_0^{t_2}\), which leaves the integral over \([t_1,t_2]\). The limits of integration are the two dates on the calendar of the force, not 0 and the length of the interval.

The average force. Dividing the exponent of (3.13) by the length \(t_2-t_1\) gives the average force over the interval, \(\bar\delta=\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta_r\,dr\). A constant force \(\bar\delta\) produces the same growth over \([t_1,t_2]\), and the equivalent effective annual rate for that interval is \(e^{\bar\delta}-1\). The average depends on where the interval lies, which is the subject of the next section.

Discounting with a variable force L9 24:41

Derivation. Accumulation multiplies by \(\exp\int\delta_r\,dr\); discounting divides by it. To find the present value at time 0 of an amount \(\mathrm{FV}\) due at time \(T\), solve \(\mathrm{FV}=\mathrm{PV}\exp\int_0^T\delta_r\,dr\) for \(\mathrm{PV}\), as the slide does by multiplying both sides by \(e\) raised to minus the integral:

\[ \mathrm{PV}=\mathrm{FV}\cdot\exp\Bigl(-\int_0^T\delta_r\,dr\Bigr),\qquad v(t)=\exp\Bigl(-\int_0^t\delta_r\,dr\Bigr)=\frac{1}{a(t)}. \tag{3.14} \]

The second formula is the discount function of the summary slide, the value at 0 of 1 due at \(t\). For a constant force, \(v(t)=e^{-\delta t}=v^t\), with \(v=1/(1+i)\) the discount factor of the earlier weeks; the argument in parentheses distinguishes the function \(v(t)\) from the number \(v\). The present value at \(t_1\) of an amount due at \(t_2\) is, by (3.13), \(\mathrm{FV}\exp(-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta_r\,dr)\), with the same rule for the limits as in accumulation.

Interpretation. The slide describes the integral as the total "resistance" or "cost" of time from 0 to \(T\); that is a metaphor. In terms of the model, the integral is the logarithm of the accumulation factor over \([0,T]\): an amount due at \(T\) is worth less at 0 by the factor that 1 at time 0 would have grown by.

from fractions import Fraction as Fr
from math import exp, log


def delta_from_i(i):
    """Constant force equivalent to the effective annual rate i (3.5)."""
    if i <= -1:
        raise ValueError(f"need i > -1: {i}")
    return log(1 + i)


def i_nom(i, m):
    """Nominal rate of interest convertible m-thly (3.2)."""
    return m*((1 + i)**(1/m) - 1)


def d_nom(i, m):
    """Nominal rate of discount convertible m-thly (3.3)."""
    return m*(1 - (1 + i)**(-1/m))


def simpson(f, t1, t2, n=2):
    """Composite Simpson rule, n (even) subintervals; exact for cubics."""
    if n <= 0 or n % 2:
        raise ValueError(f"need an even n > 0: {n}")
    h = (t2 - t1)/Fr(n)
    inner = sum((4 if k % 2 else 2)*f(t1 + k*h) for k in range(1, n))
    return (f(t1) + inner + f(t2))*h/3


def force_integral(pieces, t1, t2, n=2):
    """Integral of a piecewise force from t1 to t2 (3.13).
    pieces: (start, end, f) in time order, each f smooth on its piece."""
    if t1 > t2:
        raise ValueError(f"need t1 <= t2: {t1}, {t2}")
    ends = [(a, b) for a, b, _ in pieces]
    gaps = any(b != a2 for (_, b), (a2, _) in zip(ends, ends[1:]))
    if gaps or ends[0][0] > t1 or ends[-1][1] < t2:
        raise ValueError("pieces do not cover [t1, t2] without gaps")
    total = 0
    for a, b, f in pieces:
        lo, hi = max(a, t1), min(b, t2)
        if lo < hi:
            total += simpson(f, lo, hi, n)
    return total

What these results establish and what they do not

Within their assumptions, the results of this chapter are exact. The limit (3.4), the relation (3.5), the ordering (3.8) and the formula (3.12) are theorems about the functions defined in (3.1)–(3.3) and (3.10): (3.4) and (3.5) follow from the limit \((e^u-1)/u\to1\) and the lemma (3.6), (3.8) from the integral representations (3.9), and (3.12) from the differential equation (3.11) and its uniqueness. The program confirms (3.5), (3.8) and (3.12) numerically; it does not prove them.

The assumptions are of three kinds. Definitions: the force is \(A'(t)/A(t)\), the accumulation function starts at \(a(0)=1\), and a constant force \(\delta\) is equivalent to the effective rate \(e^\delta-1\). A model: growth is continuous in time, with no jumps in the balance, and the force is a known function of time, continuous or with finitely many jumps; the same force applies to every amount invested at the same date. A convention: time is measured in years and every force is per year; a force per month or per day must be converted before it is integrated against years.

The results stop applying in identifiable places. A balance that is credited only at discrete dates, like the staircase of Figure 3.1, has no force at the credit dates, and its value between credit dates is not \((1+i)^t\). With a varying force, there is no single \(\delta\) with \(1+i=e^\delta\) for all years: if \(i\) is the effective rate measured over one particular year, \(\ln(1+i)\) is the average force over that year, not the force at any instant of it, and the equivalent effective rate of another interval is \(e^{\bar\delta}-1\) for the average force over that interval. The value of an amount invested from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\) depends on the dates themselves, not only on \(t_2-t_1\), unless the force is constant. The model takes the future force as known; it says nothing about how actual rates move, and the lecture's references to central banks and market volatility are illustrations, not evidence used here.

The force of interest, the conversion between it and the effective and nominal rates, and time-value equations "involving variable force of interest" are named learning outcomes of the Society of Actuaries Exam FM (SOA Exam FM syllabus, October 2026, p. 2). The lecture names Exam FM as the examination for which the conversion skill matters; the recordings do not state that the course is a preparation for it.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). Under simple discount at rate \(d\), the accumulation function is \(a(t)=1/(1-dt)\) for \(0\le t\lt1/d\). (a) Derive the force \(\delta_t\). (b) Compare it with the force \(i/(1+it)\) of simple interest: which one rises and which one falls with \(t\)? (c) Check (3.12) for the simple-discount force over \([0,5]\) with \(d=8\%\). Round to six decimals.

Solution

(a) Write \(a(t)=(1-dt)^{-1}\), where \(d\) is the rate of simple discount. Its derivative is \(a'(t)=d\,(1-dt)^{-2}\), so by (3.10) \(\delta_t=\frac{a'(t)}{a(t)}=\frac{d\,(1-dt)^{-2}}{(1-dt)^{-1}}=\frac{d}{1-dt}\) for \(0\le t\lt1/d\). (The derivative is written with a prime so that the letter \(d\) means only the rate in this display.)

(b) The simple-discount force \(d/(1-dt)\) has a denominator that falls with \(t\), so the force rises, without bound as \(t\to1/d\). The simple-interest force \(i/(1+it)\) has a denominator that rises, so the force falls. With \(d=i=8\%\), both start at 0.08 at \(t=0\); at \(t=5\) the simple-discount force is \(0.08/0.6=0.133333\) and the simple-interest force \(0.08/1.4=0.057143\); at \(t=10\) they are 0.400000 and 0.044444. Compound interest, with a constant force, lies between the two models.

(c) \(\int_0^5\frac{0.08}{1-0.08r}\,dr=\bigl[-\ln(1-0.08r)\bigr]_0^5=-\ln0.6=0.510826\), and \(e^{0.510826}=1.666667=1/0.6=a(5)\). Formula (3.12) returns the given accumulation function.

Exercise 2 (exam-style computation). The force of interest is \(\delta_t=0.02+0.01t\) for \(t\ge0\). A deposit of 1,000 dollars is made at \(t=1\). Calculate its value at \(t=4\), rounded to the nearest cent, with no intermediate rounding. Choose the closest answer: (A) 1,110.71 (B) 1,135.00 (C) 1,144.54 (D) 1,173.51 (E) 1,197.22.

Solution

By (3.13), the exponent is \(\int_1^4(0.02+0.01r)\,dr=0.02\times3+0.005(16-1)=0.06+0.075=0.135\), and the value is \(1000\,e^{0.135}=1{,}144.54\): answer (C).

Each wrong choice comes from a specific error. (A) integrates from 0 to 3, the integration-limit trap: \(1000\,e^{0.105}\). (B) adds the exponent as simple interest, \(1000(1+0.135)\). (D) integrates from 0 to 4, as if the deposit had been made at \(t=0\): \(1000\,e^{0.16}\). (E) uses the force at the withdrawal date, \(\delta_4=0.06\), for all three years: \(1000\,e^{0.18}\). For a linear force the exact exponent equals the force at the midpoint of the interval times its length, \(\delta_{2.5}\times3=0.045\times3=0.135\), a quick check.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). A review question of the course asks which of four statements about interest rates is false for \(i\gt0\). Take the force of the step example of this chapter, 0.04 for \(0\le t\lt3\) and 0.06 for \(3\le t\le5\), and decide for each statement whether it holds for this force. Give a number for each statement that fails. (a) The force equals \(\ln(1+i)\) for one fixed effective rate \(i\). (b) The accumulation function is \(e^{\delta t}\) for one number \(\delta\). (c) The growth factor from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\) depends only on \(t_2-t_1\). (d) \(v(t)\,a(t)=1\) for every \(t\). (e) \(a(t)\gt0\) for every \(t\). Round to six decimals.

Solution

(a) Fails. The force is \(\ln1.040811\) on \([0,3)\) and \(\ln1.061837\) on \([3,5]\); no single \(i\) gives both. The effective rate over the first year is 4.0811% and over the fifth year 6.1837%.

(b) Fails. If \(a(t)=e^{\delta t}\), then \(a(3)=e^{0.12}\) forces \(\delta=0.04\), but then \(a(5)=e^{0.2}=1.221403\), while the true value is \(e^{0.24}=1.271249\).

(c) Fails. From 0 to 1 the factor is \(e^{0.04}=1.040811\); from 4 to 5, an interval of the same length, it is \(e^{0.06}=1.061837\).

(d) Holds, by (3.14): \(v(t)=1/a(t)\) for every force. At \(t=5\), \(v(5)=e^{-0.24}=0.786628\) and \(a(5)v(5)=1\).

(e) Holds, by the uniqueness argument after (3.12): \(a(t)\) is an exponential, positive for every force, including a negative one.

Statements (a), (b) and (c) are properties of a constant force only. They are true in the review question's setting, where the force is constant, and they fail as soon as the force varies.

Exercise 4 (counterexample and proof). A student claims: "With a step force, only the rates and their durations matter, not their order." Compare force F1 (0.04 on \([0,3)\), 0.06 on \([3,5]\)) with force F2 (0.06 on \([0,2)\), 0.04 on \([2,5]\)). (a) Show that 1 invested at \(t=0\) has the same value at \(t=5\) under both forces, and prove that this holds for any rearrangement of the pieces over \([0,5]\). (b) Show that 100,000 dollars invested at \(t=2\) and withdrawn at \(t=5\) has different values under F1 and F2. (c) State the correct version of the student's claim. Round amounts to cents and factors to six decimals.

Solution

(a) Under F1, \(\int_0^5\delta_r\,dr=0.04\times3+0.06\times2=0.24\); under F2, \(0.06\times2+0.04\times3=0.24\). Both give \(a(5)=e^{0.24}=1.271249\). In general, the integral over \([0,5]\) of a step force is the sum of the products force times duration, and a sum does not depend on the order of its terms; so any rearrangement of the pieces leaves \(a(5)\) unchanged.

(b) Under F1 the deposit sees 0.04 for one year and 0.06 for two: exponent \(0.04+0.12=0.16\), value \(100{,}000\,e^{0.16}=117{,}351.09\). Under F2 it sees 0.04 for three years: exponent 0.12, value \(100{,}000\,e^{0.12}=112{,}749.69\). The difference is 4,601.40 dollars.

(c) The order of the pieces does not matter for an amount invested over the whole interval on which the force is rearranged. It matters for every amount invested over part of that interval, because (3.13) integrates only over the dates the amount is invested. The same holds for discounting: the present value at 0 of 1 due at \(t=3\) is \(e^{-0.12}=0.886920\) under F1 and \(e^{-0.16}=0.852144\) under F2.

Exercise 5 (computation, focal dates). The force is 0.05 for \(0\le t\lt4\) and 0.07 for \(t\ge4\). Payments of 10,000 dollars at \(t=2\) and 20,000 dollars at \(t=6\) are due. (a) Find their present value at \(t=0\). (b) Find their value at the focal date \(t=4\), moving each payment separately, and check that it equals the answer to (a) times \(a(4)\). (c) A student discounts the second payment at 0.07 for all six years. How large is the error? Round amounts to cents.

Solution

(a) By (3.14), the first payment is discounted over \([0,2]\) with exponent \(0.05\times2=0.1\): \(10{,}000\,e^{-0.1}=9{,}048.37\). The second is discounted over \([0,6]\), split at 4: exponent \(0.05\times4+0.07\times2=0.34\), so \(20{,}000\,e^{-0.34}=14{,}235.41\). The present value is 23,283.78 dollars.

(b) The first payment is accumulated from 2 to 4 with exponent 0.1: \(10{,}000\,e^{0.1}=11{,}051.71\). The second is discounted from 6 to 4 with exponent 0.14: \(20{,}000\,e^{-0.14}=17{,}387.16\). The sum is 28,438.87 dollars. Also \(a(4)=e^{0.2}\), and \(23{,}283.78\times e^{0.2}=28{,}438.87\) (from the unrounded present value). The two focal dates agree, as the section on discounting showed they must.

(c) The student's value is \(20{,}000\,e^{-0.42}=13{,}140.94\), which is 1,094.47 dollars below the correct 14,235.41: it applies the later force to the first four years, where the force is 0.05.

Exercise 6 (inversion and judgment). Take the lecture's accumulation function \(a(t)=1+0.05t^2\). (a) Find the time at which its force is largest, and the largest force. (b) A colleague says: "The balance grows by more each year, so its force of interest rises every year." Judge the statement with numbers. (c) Is there an effective annual rate \(i\) for which \(a(t)=(1+i)^t\)? Round to six decimals.

Solution

(a) \(\delta_t=0.1t/(1+0.05t^2)\). Its derivative has the sign of \(0.1(1+0.05t^2)-0.1t\times0.1t=0.1-0.005t^2\), which is positive for \(t\lt\sqrt{20}\) and negative after. The maximum is at \(t=\sqrt{20}=4.472136\), where \(\delta_t=0.1\times4.472136/(1+1)=0.223607\).

(b) The first half of the statement is true: \(a'(t)=0.1t\) increases, so the yearly increments grow. The second half is false after \(t=4.472136\): \(\delta_{10}=0.166667\) and \(\delta_{20}=0.095238\), both below the maximum. The force is growth relative to the balance; the balance grows quadratically, while growth in proportion to the balance would make it grow exponentially. An increasing \(a'(t)\) does not imply an increasing \(a'(t)/a(t)\).

(c) No. Compound interest has a constant force, \(\ln(1+i)\), by (3.10); this function's force is 0 at \(t=0\) and changes with \(t\). Equivalently, \(a(1)=1.05\) would require \(i=5\%\), but then \(a(2)\) would be \(1.1025\), not \(1.2\).
