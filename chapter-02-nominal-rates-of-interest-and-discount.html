Nominal rates of interest and of discount

A small business needs 500,000 dollars now and will repay the loan in one sum after 5 years. Four lenders quote four rates, invented for this example: lender A quotes 10% a year, effective; lender B quotes 9.6% a year compounded monthly; lender C quotes 9.8% a year compounded half-yearly; lender D quotes a rate of discount of 9.5% a year, convertible quarterly. Ranked by the quoted number, D looks cheapest and A dearest. The results of this chapter rank them in exactly the opposite order. The amounts owed after 5 years are 805,255.00 dollars to A, 806,495.47 to B, 806,723.83 to C and 808,628.54 to D. The lowest quote costs 3,373.54 dollars more than the highest, and its interest, 308,628.54 dollars, is 1.1% more than A's 305,255.00. The quoted numbers are not comparable as they stand: only A is an effective rate, and the other three are nominal rates, each with its own frequency and its own timing of interest. This chapter defines the nominal rate of interest \(i^{(m)}\) and the nominal rate of discount \(d^{(m)}\), derives the equations that convert each of them to the effective annual rate, proves the order in which all of these rates lie for one effective rate, and uses the conversions to compare quotes that are made in different conventions.

The running case and the tools from earlier in the course L6 0:12

Earlier lectures of the course built the effective rate of interest, the effective rate of discount and the compound model of growth. This chapter uses them to compare rates that are quoted in different ways. This section restates them. Each is taken as given from earlier in the course; none is derived again here.

Accumulation function and effective rate. Time \(t\) is measured in years. The accumulation function \(a(t)\) is the value at time \(t\) of one unit invested at time 0, so \(a(0)=1\). An investment of \(k\) dollars at time 0 has the value \(A(t)=k\,a(t)\) at time \(t\); \(A\) is the amount function, and \(k\) is the principal (the letter on the slides of Lecture 6). The effective annual rate of interest is \(i=a(1)-1\): the interest that one unit earns in one year, paid at the end of the year. Under compound interest at a constant rate,

\[ a(t)=(1+i)^t\qquad\text{for every } t\ge0, \tag{2.1} \]

including fractional \(t\). Compound interest means that interest, once credited, earns interest in later periods. All rates in this chapter satisfy \(i\gt-1\), so that \(1+i\gt0\); the sections say where \(i\gt0\) is needed.

Discount factor and effective rate of discount. The discount factor \(v=1/(1+i)\) is the value at time 0 of one unit due at time 1. The effective annual rate of discount \(d\) is interest paid in advance: a lender who will be repaid 1 at the end of the year hands over \(1-d\) at the start, so \(d\) is the interest as a fraction of the end amount. Since \(1-d\) at time 0 and 1 at time 1 have the same value, \(1-d=v\), and

\[ v=\frac1{1+i}=1-d,\qquad d=\frac{i}{1+i}=iv,\qquad 1+i=(1-d)^{-1}. \tag{2.2} \]

For \(i\gt0\), \(0\lt d\lt i\). At 10%, \(d=0.1/1.1=9.0909\%\).

Rounding. Every computation in this chapter carries its intermediate values unrounded. Rates are displayed in percent to four decimals (six decimal places as a fraction) and amounts to cents, only where they are displayed. A displayed equation that shows a rounded rate beside a result therefore shows a rounding: for example, \(4\times0.024114=0.096456\), while the unrounded value is 0.0964548, displayed as 9.6455%. The program at the end of the chapter reproduces, from unrounded values, the running case, Table 2.2 and the lecture and open-source examples. The other displayed values and the values in the exercises follow from the same formulas with unrounded intermediates; the program does not print them.

The running case. A business borrows 500,000 dollars at \(t=0\) and repays everything in one sum at \(t=5\). Four lenders quote the rates of the lead: A, 10% effective (the lender labels A to D are names, not the function \(A(t)\)); B, 9.6% compounded monthly; C, 9.8% compounded half-yearly; D, 9.5% rate of discount convertible quarterly. The question is which offer is cheapest, and by how much. For a single repayment the answer depends only on the growth factor of one year, \(1+i\), because by (2.1) the amount owed at \(t=5\) is \(500{,}000(1+i)^5\). Offer A gives \(1+i=1.1\) and an amount owed of \(500{,}000\times1.1^5=805{,}255.00\) dollars. The other three quotes do not state \(1+i\). The formal model of the chapter is a year cut into \(m\) equal periods, a rate charged in each period (at its end or at its start), and the growth factor of the whole year that results. Each section fixes one of these choices and finds the factor \(1+i\).

A quoted rate and an earned rate: the bank advertisement L6 2:39

A bank advertises that it pays 12% interest, compounded monthly. The question is whether a depositor earns 12% a year. The answer is no. The bank divides the 12% by the 12 months and credits 1% at the end of each month. The interest of each month is added to the balance and earns 1% in every later month. After 12 months one unit has grown to

\[ 1.01^{12}=1.126825, \]

so the depositor earns 12.6825% in the year, which the slide displays as 12.68%. The difference, 0.6825 percentage points, is interest on interest credited within the year. A deposit of 10,000 dollars for one year earns 1,268.25 dollars at this bank, against the 1,200 dollars that the number 12 suggests.

Two numbers describe this account. The number 12% is the one in the advertisement. The number 12.6825% is the growth of money over one year, the effective annual rate \(i\) of the previous section. The rest of the chapter needs notation that keeps the two apart, and an equation that turns the first into the second for any frequency. The calculation uses only (2.1) applied per month: one unit at 1% a month for 12 months grows by \(1.01^{12}\). It assumes that the 1% is credited every month and left in the account; if the depositor withdrew the interest each month, the balance would not compound and the year's interest would be 12%.

Nominal rate of interest, frequency and periodic rate L6 7:26

The slide separates three objects. The effective rate \(i\) is the actual growth of money over one year (12.68% in the advertisement). The frequency \(m\) is the number of compounding periods in a year, a positive whole number: \(m=12\) for monthly, \(m=4\) for quarterly, \(m=2\) for half-yearly, \(m=1\) for yearly. The nominal rate of interest convertible m-thly, written \(i^{(m)}\), is the annual rate that is quoted, a rate in name only in the slide's description (12% in the advertisement, so \(i^{(12)}=12\%\)). A nominal rate is incomplete without its frequency.

The slide's trap alert is notational: the superscript \((m)\) is a label and not an exponent. \(i^{(12)}\) is a rate with the label 12. It is not \(i\) raised to the power 12; at \(i=10\%\), \(i^{12}=10^{-12}\), while \(i^{(12)}=9.5690\%\), as the next sections show.

The definition that connects the quote to the money is what the slide calls the golden rule of nominal rates:

\[ \text{effective rate per period of } \tfrac1m \text{ year} = \frac{i^{(m)}}{m}. \tag{2.3} \]

One unit at the start of a period of length \(1/m\) year grows to \(1+i^{(m)}/m\) at its end (the slide's timeline from 0 to \(1/m\)). In the advertisement, \(i^{(12)}/12=12\%/12=1\%\) a month. The slide's summary calls this periodic rate the engine of every calculation: every calculation runs on \(i^{(m)}/m\), never on \(i^{(m)}\) itself. Equation (2.3) is the definition of \(i^{(m)}\); it is the convention by which a lender states a rate, not a result. This book writes \(j\) for the effective rate per period when a symbol is needed, so \(j=i^{(m)}/m\) and \(i^{(m)}=mj\).

The words for the frequency vary. A slide later in Lecture 6 lists 12% compounded monthly, 12% convertible monthly and 12% payable monthly as three phrasings of the same rate, \(i^{(12)}=0.12\). This chapter uses the words compounded and convertible interchangeably, as the lecture does. For \(m=1\), (2.3) says the rate per year is \(i^{(1)}\), so \(i^{(1)}=i\): a rate convertible once a year is the effective annual rate.

The bridge equation for nominal interest L6 12:53

Question. Given \(i^{(m)}\) and \(m\), what is the effective annual rate \(i\)? And given \(i\) and \(m\), what nominal rate must be quoted?

Derivation. The slide follows one unit for one year along two paths. Path A (effective): by the definition of \(i\), \(a(1)=1+i\). Path B (nominal): the year is cut into \(m\) periods. At the end of the first period the unit has grown to \(1+i^{(m)}/m\) by (2.3). In the second period this whole balance earns \(i^{(m)}/m\), so it becomes \((1+i^{(m)}/m)+(1+i^{(m)}/m)\,i^{(m)}/m=(1+i^{(m)}/m)^2\); the slide takes the common factor \(1+i^{(m)}/m\) out of the two terms. Each period multiplies the balance by the same factor, and after the \(m\)th period, at the end of the year, \(a(1)=(1+i^{(m)}/m)^m\). The two paths describe the same money over the same year, so their values at \(t=1\) are equal. The slide calls the result the fundamental link, and the lecture calls it the bridge equation:

\[ 1+i=\Bigl(1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^m,\qquad i=\Bigl(1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^m-1. \tag{2.4} \]

The hinge is the second period: the interest of the first period is in the balance on which the second period's interest is computed. That is the compound assumption of (2.1), applied to periods of \(1/m\) year.

The inverse. Solving (2.4) for \(i^{(m)}\) takes two steps, and the order matters. Take the \(m\)th root of both sides, which is allowed because both are positive: \((1+i)^{1/m}=1+i^{(m)}/m\). Subtract 1 and multiply by \(m\):

\[ i^{(m)}=m\bigl[(1+i)^{1/m}-1\bigr]. \tag{2.5} \]

The bracket is the effective rate for \(1/m\) year under (2.1), since one unit grows to \((1+i)^{1/m}\) in \(1/m\) year. So (2.5) says again that \(i^{(m)}\) is \(m\) times the effective rate per period: (2.3), read in reverse.

Checks. One period a year: with \(m=1\), (2.4) gives \(1+i=1+i^{(1)}\), so \(i^{(1)}=i\), as the previous section found from (2.3). The advertisement: \(i^{(12)}=0.12\) gives \(i=1.01^{12}-1=12.6825\%\). Round trip: substituting (2.5) into (2.4) gives \((1+(1+i)^{1/m}-1)^m-1=i\); the program checks the round trip numerically for eight frequencies. Zero rate: \(i=0\) gives \(i^{(m)}=0\) for every \(m\).

The running case, offers B and C. Offer B quotes \(i^{(12)}=9.6\%\), so the monthly rate is \(0.096/12=0.8\%\) and

\[ 1+i_B=1.008^{12}=1.100339,\qquad i_B=10.0339\%. \]

Offer C quotes \(i^{(2)}=9.8\%\), so the half-yearly rate is 4.9% and \(1+i_C=1.049^2=1.100401\), \(i_C=10.0401\%\). Both quoted numbers are below 10%, and both effective rates are above it. The amounts owed after five years are \(500{,}000\times1.008^{60}=806{,}495.47\) and \(500{,}000\times1.049^{10}=806{,}723.83\) dollars, against 805,255.00 for offer A. Both exponents count periods: 60 months and 10 half-years.

Boundary. Equation (2.4) assumes that interest is credited at the end of each of the \(m\) periods and is reinvested at the same rate for the rest of the year. It needs \(1+i^{(m)}/m\gt0\). It does not say how interest is paid between the period ends; for a deposit withdrawn in the middle of a month, a bank's own rule decides.

A chord lemma. Let \(g\) be twice differentiable on an interval \([0,Y)\) with \(g(0)=0\). If \(g''\gt0\) on \((0,Y)\), then the chord slope \(g(y)/y\) strictly increases on \((0,Y)\); if \(g''\lt0\), it strictly decreases. Proof: the derivative of \(g(y)/y\) is \(Q(y)/y^2\) with \(Q(y)=y\,g'(y)-g(y)\). Then \(Q(0)=0\) and \(Q'(y)=g'(y)+y\,g''(y)-g'(y)=y\,g''(y)\), which has the sign of \(g''\) for \(y\gt0\). So \(Q\), and with it the derivative of \(g(y)/y\), has the sign of \(g''\) on \((0,Y)\). ∎ The limit of the chord slope as \(y\to0\) is \(g'(0)\), by the definition of the derivative. Every function to which this chapter applies the lemma is defined for all \(y\ge0\), so \(Y\) may be taken as large as needed; in Exercise 4 one function is defined only for \(y\lt1/x\), and there \(Y=1/x\).

Monotone in \(m\). Fix a nominal rate \(x\gt0\). The effective rate at frequency \(m\) is \((1+x/m)^m-1\). Its logarithm, \(m\ln(1+x/m)\), can be written as \(\ln(1+xy)/y\) with \(y=1/m\). The function \(\ln(1+xy)\) is zero at \(y=0\) and strictly concave in \(y\), and for such a function the slope \(\ln(1+xy)/y\) of the chord from the origin decreases as \(y\) grows. (This is the chord lemma above with \(g(y)=\ln(1+xy)\), \(g''(y)=-x^2/(1+xy)^2\lt0\).) A larger \(m\) is a smaller \(y\), so the logarithm, and with it the effective rate, increases with \(m\). The comparison runs over all whole numbers \(m\), not only over the frequencies in the table.

The limit. As \(m\to\infty\), put \(u=x/m\to0\). Then \(m\ln(1+x/m)=x\cdot\ln(1+u)/u\), and \(\ln(1+u)/u\to1\), because the derivative of \(\ln\) at 1 is 1. Hence

\[ \lim_{m\to\infty}\Bigl(1+\frac{x}{m}\Bigr)^m=e^x. \tag{2.6} \]

By (2.6) and the monotonicity above, at a nominal 12% the effective rate increases with \(m\) toward \(e^{0.12}-1=12.7497\%\) and never reaches it. Daily compounding, at 12.7475%, is 0.0022 percentage points below the limit. Compared with compounding once a year, where the effective rate is \(x\) itself, more frequent compounding at a fixed quote \(x\) raises the effective rate by less than \(e^{x}-1-x\), which is 0.7497 percentage points at 12%.

Interpretation. A lender who keeps the quoted number and compounds more often charges more. A borrower who compares two quotes with the same number and different frequencies should expect the more frequent one to cost more; this comparison holds for nominal rates of interest only. A quote of a rate of discount behaves in the opposite way: at a fixed quoted number, more frequent conversion lowers its effective rate (Exercise 4 proves both directions with the chord lemma of this section).

A nominal rate for a given effective rate, and the calculator worksheet L6 19:25

Growth over t years, and the words for compounding L6 26:38

Over \(t\) years there are \(N=mt\) periods, each multiplying the balance by \(1+i^{(m)}/m\). The slide's amount function is

\[ A(t)=k\Bigl(1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^{mt}. \tag{2.7} \]

By (2.4), \((1+i^{(m)}/m)^{mt}=((1+i^{(m)}/m)^m)^t=(1+i)^t\), so (2.7) is (2.1) with the principal \(k\): converting the quote to an effective rate first and compounding in years gives the same amount as compounding in periods. When \(mt\) is a whole number, (2.7) counts whole periods. When it is not, (2.7) still equals \(k(1+i)^t\) under the compound model (2.1), but a bank that credits interest only at the end of each period may pay a different amount for the fraction of a period; the formula then states the model, not the bank's rule.

The lecture's example. Invest 1,000 dollars for 5 years at 8% compounded quarterly. Then \(m=4\), \(t=5\), there are 20 periods, and the rate per period is \(8\%/4=2\%\):

\[ A(5)=1000\times1.02^{20}=1{,}485.95 . \]

The effective annual rate is \(1.02^4-1=8.2432\%\), and \(1000\times1.082432^{5}\) gives the same 1,485.95.

The running case, offer D in waiting. Offers A, B and C now have effective rates: 10%, 10.0339% and 10.0401%. Their amounts owed after five years follow from (2.7) with \(k=500{,}000\): \(500{,}000\times1.1^5\), \(500{,}000\times1.008^{60}\) and \(500{,}000\times1.049^{10}\). Offer D quotes a rate of discount, which (2.7) does not cover; the following sections define it.

The words. A rate quoted with no frequency at all is ambiguous: it may be an effective annual rate, or a nominal rate whose frequency the writer took for granted. Exercise 3 measures how much the reading changes the answer.

The ordering of the nominal rates of interest L6 29:58

The previous sections held the nominal rate fixed. This one holds the effective rate fixed and asks how the equivalent nominal rates compare. The slide states, for a fixed effective rate \(i\gt0\),

\[ i^{(1)}\gt i^{(2)}\gt i^{(4)}\gt i^{(12)}\gt\cdots, \]

with the reason that a rate compounded more often does not need to start as high to reach the same final result: the stronger the compounding, the lower the nominal rate it requires. At \(i=10\%\), (2.5) gives \(i^{(1)}=10\%\), \(i^{(2)}=9.7618\%\), \(i^{(4)}=9.6455\%\) and \(i^{(12)}=9.5690\%\).

This is the same fact as the previous section's monotonicity, seen from the other side: if \((1+x/m)^m\) increases with \(m\) for every fixed \(x\), then a larger \(m\) needs a smaller \(x\) to give the same \(1+i\). The full statement, with the nominal rates of discount on the same line and the conditions on \(i\) and \(m\), is proved once in the section on the hierarchy of rates, after \(d^{(m)}\) is defined.

The lecture adds that credit-card lenders quote a low nominal rate and charge a higher effective one. That is the speaker's judgment about practice, given without data or a source, and this book does not use it as a finding. What the mathematics establishes is narrower: for \(i\gt0\) and \(m\gt1\), \(i^{(m)}\lt i\), so any nominal rate of interest with \(m\gt1\) is a smaller number than the effective rate it produces.

Nominal rate of discount: interest taken in advance m times a year L7 3:56

A nominal rate of interest charges interest at the end of each period. The lecture recalls the alternative from the effective rate of discount: a bank asked for a loan of 100 dollars takes 5 dollars of interest at once and hands over 95. Interest taken in advance at the start of each period, \(m\) times a year, defines the nominal rate of discount convertible m-thly, written \(d^{(m)}\). The slide's example is \(d^{(12)}=12\%\): the bank cuts 1% from the amount at the start of every month, which the slide calls a haircut.

The definition, which the slide calls Golden Rule 2, is the counterpart of (2.3):

\[ \text{effective rate of discount per period of } \tfrac1m \text{ year} = \frac{d^{(m)}}{m}. \tag{2.8} \]

One unit due at the end of a period of \(1/m\) year is worth \(1-d^{(m)}/m\) at its start (the slide's timeline from \(1/m\) back to 0). The 1% haircut is 1% of the amount due at the end of the month, not 1% of the amount handed over: a borrower who will owe 100 at the end of the month receives 99 at its start, and the interest of 1 is \(1/99=1.0101\%\) of the amount received. The slide adds that this factor moves money backward by \(1/m\) year. As with \(i^{(m)}\), the superscript is a label; \(d^{(1)}=d\), because a discount taken once a year in advance is the effective annual rate of discount.

The rate \(d^{(m)}/m\) must be less than 1 for the definition to make sense: a discount of the whole amount due would hand over nothing. For \(d^{(m)}\gt0\) the lender earns interest; the sign conventions mirror those of \(i^{(m)}\).

The bridge equation for discount L7 8:17

Question. Given \(d^{(m)}\) and \(m\), what is the effective annual rate of discount \(d\), and what is the effective annual rate of interest \(i\)?

Derivation. The slide follows the present value of one unit due at \(t=1\) along two paths. Path A (effective): by (2.2), the value at \(t=0\) is \(v=1-d\). Path B (nominal): move the unit back one period at a time. By (2.8), one unit at \(t=1\) is worth \(1-d^{(m)}/m\) at \(t=1-1/m\). That amount is in turn worth \((1-d^{(m)}/m)\) times itself one period earlier, because (2.8) applies to any amount due at the end of a period (an amount due at the end is worth that amount times the value of 1). After \(m\) steps back, at \(t=0\), the value is \((1-d^{(m)}/m)^m\). Both paths give the value at \(t=0\) of the same payment, so

\[ 1-d=\Bigl(1-\frac{d^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^m,\qquad 1+i=\Bigl(1-\frac{d^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^{-m}. \tag{2.9} \]

The second form follows from the first and \(1+i=(1-d)^{-1}\) in (2.2). The lecture's memory aid is that (2.9) is (2.4) with \(+i\) replaced by \(-d\), in the effective rate and in the nominal rate alike. The inverse, by the same two steps as (2.5) (take the \(m\)th root, then isolate the rate), is

\[ d^{(m)}=m\bigl[1-(1-d)^{1/m}\bigr]=m\bigl[1-(1+i)^{-1/m}\bigr]=m\bigl(1-v^{1/m}\bigr). \tag{2.10} \]

The bracket \(1-v^{1/m}\) is the effective rate of discount for \(1/m\) year under (2.1): one unit due in \(1/m\) year is worth \(v^{1/m}\) now.

Checks. With \(m=1\), (2.9) gives \(1-d=1-d^{(1)}\), so \(d^{(1)}=d\). With \(i=0\), \(v=1\) and \(d^{(m)}=0\). The slide's example \(d^{(12)}=12\%\) gives \(1+i=0.99^{-12}=1.128178\), an effective rate of interest of 12.8178%, larger than the 12.6825% of \(i^{(12)}=12\%\): the same quoted number costs more as a discount rate than as an interest rate.

The running case, offer D. Offer D quotes \(d^{(4)}=9.5\%\), so the lender takes \(9.5\%/4=2.375\%\) of the amount due at the end of each quarter in advance. By (2.9),

\[ 1+i_D=(1-0.02375)^{-4}=1.100920,\qquad i_D=10.0920\%. \]

The lowest quote of the four has the highest effective rate. The amount owed after five years, 20 quarters, is \(500{,}000\times0.97625^{-20}=808{,}628.54\) dollars.

Boundary. Equation (2.9) assumes that the discount of each period is computed on the amount due at the end of that period, \(m\) times a year at equal intervals, with the same rate throughout. The chain (2.12) contains three claims: \(i^{(m)}\) decreases as \(m\) increases; \(d^{(m)}\) increases as \(m\) increases; and every rate of discount is below every rate of interest. The proof below establishes all three, for every whole number \(m\ge1\) (not only the frequencies on the slide), and states where \(i\gt0\) is used.

The tool. The chord lemma of the section on the frequency of compounding applies: a twice-differentiable \(g\) with \(g(0)=0\) has a chord slope \(g(y)/y\) that strictly increases on \((0,Y)\) when \(g''\gt0\) there and strictly decreases when \(g''\lt0\), and the slope tends to \(g'(0)\) as \(y\to0\).

Proof of (2.12). Let \(c=\ln(1+i)\), so that \(1+i=e^{c}\), and let \(y=1/m\). By (2.5) and (2.10),

\[ i^{(m)}=\frac{e^{cy}-1}{y},\qquad d^{(m)}=\frac{1-e^{-cy}}{y}. \]

Both are chord slopes from the origin. For \(g_1(y)=e^{cy}-1\), \(g_1''(y)=c^2e^{cy}\), which is positive when \(c\neq0\), that is when \(i\neq0\). By the lemma, \(i^{(m)}\) strictly increases in \(y\), so it strictly decreases as \(m\) increases. For \(g_2(y)=1-e^{-cy}\), \(g_2''(y)=-c^2e^{-cy}\lt0\) when \(i\neq0\), so \(d^{(m)}\) strictly decreases in \(y\) and strictly increases as \(m\) increases. Both chord slopes tend to the same limit as \(y\to0\), \(g_1'(0)=g_2'(0)=c\). A strictly decreasing sequence lies strictly above its limit, and a strictly increasing one strictly below it, so for every \(m\) and \(p\),

\[ d=d^{(1)}\lt d^{(p)}\lt\ln(1+i)\lt i^{(m)}\lt i^{(1)}=i\qquad(p,m\gt1,\ i\gt-1,\ i\neq0), \tag{2.13} \]

and the inequalities \(d\le d^{(p)}\) and \(i^{(m)}\le i\) hold with equality only at \(p=1\) or \(m=1\). ∎

The proof uses only \(i\neq0\) and \(i\gt-1\): for a negative rate, \(c\lt0\), \(g_1''\) and \(g_2''\) keep their signs because \(c^2\gt0\), and the chain (2.13) holds with all the rates negative. Strictness between two frequencies needs them to differ; between a nominal rate and its effective rate it needs \(m\gt1\).

The same frequency: an identity linking the two nominal rates L7 31:17

For effective rates, (2.2) gives \(i-d=i\cdot\frac{i}{1+i}=id\). The slide states the counterpart for two nominal rates at the same frequency, \(m=p\):

\[ \frac{i^{(m)}}{m}-\frac{d^{(m)}}{m}=\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\cdot\frac{d^{(m)}}{m},\qquad 1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}=\Bigl(1-\frac{d^{(m)}}{m}\Bigr)^{-1}. \tag{2.14} \]

Derivation. Set the outer terms of (2.11) equal with \(p=m\) and take the \(m\)th root of both (positive) sides; this gives the second form, which the slide calls the periodic version of \(1+i=(1-d)^{-1}\). It is (2.2) applied to one period of \(1/m\) year, with effective rates \(i^{(m)}/m\) and \(d^{(m)}/m\) in place of \(i\) and \(d\). Write \(j=i^{(m)}/m\) and \(z=d^{(m)}/m\); the second form says \((1+j)(1-z)=1\), that is \(1+j-z-jz=1\), so \(j-z=jz\), the first form.

A reciprocal form. Divide \(j-z=jz\) by \(jz\) (both are nonzero when \(i\neq0\)): \(1/z-1/j=1\). With \(j=i^{(m)}/m\) and \(z=d^{(m)}/m\), and dividing by \(m\),

\[ \frac1{d^{(m)}}-\frac1{i^{(m)}}=\frac1m. \tag{2.15} \]

The difference of the reciprocals does not depend on the rate at all. At 10% and \(m=12\), with the unrounded rates \(d^{(12)}=0.0949327\) and \(i^{(12)}=0.0956897\), \(1/d^{(12)}-1/i^{(12)}=10.533781-10.450447=0.083333=1/12\).

Interpretation. The second form of (2.14) can be solved for either rate: \(d^{(m)}=i^{(m)}/(1+i^{(m)}/m)\).         raise ValueError(f"m must be a positive whole number: {m}")
    if growth <= 0:
        raise ValueError(f"growth factor must be positive: {growth}")

    check(p, 1 + i)

What these formulas establish and what they do not

Within their assumptions, (2.4), (2.5), (2.9), (2.10), (2.11), (2.14) and (2.15) are exact: each follows from the definitions (2.3) and (2.8) and the compound model (2.1) by algebra. The chain (2.13) is a theorem for every rate \(i\gt-1\), \(i\neq0\), and every pair of frequencies. The program confirms (2.14) and (2.15) exactly at one rate, and (2.13) and the round trips of (2.4), (2.5), (2.9) and (2.10) in floating point at two rates and eight frequencies; the proofs, not the program, establish them in general.

The assumptions are of three kinds. Definitions: \(i^{(m)}\) and \(d^{(m)}\) are defined by (2.3) and (2.8), so a nominal rate means nothing until its frequency and its timing (in arrears or in advance) are stated. A model: one constant compound rate for the whole year and for every year, so that interest credited within a year earns interest at the same rate, and (2.1) holds for fractional times. A convention: the quoted rate follows the definitions of this chapter. The Treasury bill shows a quotation that does not: its discount is simple, proportional to days, on a 360-day year, and it must be priced by its own formula (2.16) before any conversion.

The formulas stop applying where the boundary paragraphs of the bridge sections, the section on growth over \(t\) years and the section on Treasury bills say so: a rate that changes during the year makes the \(m\) factors of (2.4) different, and their product, not a power, is the growth of the year. The common limit \(\ln(1+i)\) of the nominal rates is used here only as a number; continuous compounding is a separate topic of the course.

The conversion among \(i\), \(i^{(m)}\), \(d\) and \(d^{(m)}\), with the nominal rate convertible m-thly, is a named learning outcome of the Society of Actuaries Exam FM, in its topic on the time value of money (SOA Exam FM syllabus, October 2026, p. 2). The recordings of this chapter mention examinations in general and do not name one.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). (a) Starting from (2.4) and (2.9), show that \(i^{(m)}/m-d^{(m)}/m=(i^{(m)}/m)(d^{(m)}/m)\) and that \(1/d^{(m)}-1/i^{(m)}=1/m\). (b) Show that \(d^{(m)}=i^{(m)}/(1+i^{(m)}/m)\), and explain it with a timeline of one period. (c) Check (a) and (b) at \(i=10\%\) and \(m=12\). Round rates to six decimals.

Solution

(a) Both (2.4) and (2.9) equal \(1+i\), so \((1+i^{(m)}/m)^m=(1-d^{(m)}/m)^{-m}\). Both sides are positive; taking the \(m\)th root gives \(1+j=(1-z)^{-1}\) with \(j=i^{(m)}/m\), \(z=d^{(m)}/m\). Multiply by \(1-z\): \((1+j)(1-z)=1\), so \(j-z-jz=0\), which is the first identity. For \(i\neq0\), \(j\) and \(z\) are nonzero; dividing \(j-z=jz\) by \(jz\) gives \(1/z-1/j=1\), and substituting \(z=d^{(m)}/m\), \(j=i^{(m)}/m\) gives \(m/d^{(m)}-m/i^{(m)}=1\), the second identity after division by \(m\).

(b) From \((1+j)(1-z)=1\), \(1-z=1/(1+j)\) and \(z=j/(1+j)\); multiplying by \(m\) gives \(d^{(m)}=i^{(m)}/(1+i^{(m)}/m)\). Timeline of one period of \(1/m\) year: a lender in arrears lends 1 at the start and collects \(1+j\) at the end, so the interest \(j\) is paid at the end. A lender in advance who is owed \(1+j\) at the end hands over 1 at the start and so takes \(j\) out of the amount due before lending; as a fraction of the amount due at the end, the interest taken is \(j/(1+j)=z\). The rate of discount is the same interest measured against the end amount instead of the start amount, which is the content of (2.8).

(c) At \(i=10\%\), \(m=12\): \(i^{(12)}=0.095690\), \(d^{(12)}=0.094933\). Then \(j-z=0.0000630839\) and \(jz=0.0000630839\) (the products here use the unrounded rates; with the six-decimal rates the two sides are 0.0000630833 and 0.0000630843); \(1/d^{(12)}-1/i^{(12)}=0.083333=1/12\); and \(0.095690/(1+0.095690/12)=0.094933\).

Exercise 2 (exam-style computation). A loan of 20,000 dollars is repaid in one sum after 3 years. The lender quotes a nominal rate of discount of 8% convertible monthly. Calculate the repayment, rounded to the nearest cent, with no intermediate rounding. Choose the closest answer: (A) 25,194.24 (B) 25,404.74 (C) 25,445.42 (D) 25,684.23 (E) 26,315.79.

Solution

By (2.9), \(1+i=(1-0.08/12)^{-12}\), and over 3 years the growth is \((1-0.08/12)^{-36}\). The repayment is \(20{,}000\times(0.993333\ldots)^{-36}=25{,}445.42\): answer (C).

Each wrong choice comes from one error. (A) treats 8% as an effective rate of interest, \(20{,}000\times1.08^3\). (B) treats it as a nominal rate of interest convertible monthly, \(20{,}000\times(1+0.08/12)^{36}\). (D) treats it as an effective rate of discount, \(20{,}000\times0.92^{-3}\). (E) applies simple discount for 3 years, \(20{,}000/(1-3\times0.08)\). The correct answer exceeds (B) for a reason that holds at every rate: with \(z=0.08/12\), one month's growth is \((1-z)^{-1}\) for the rate of discount and \(1+z\) for the rate of interest, and \((1-z)(1+z)=1-z^2\lt1\), so \((1-z)^{-1}\gt1+z\). For the same quoted number and frequency, the rate of discount gives the larger growth.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). A savings plan is advertised at "6% a year" with no frequency. A saver deposits 10,000 dollars for 4 years. (a) Compute the accumulated value if 6% is an effective annual rate; a nominal rate of interest convertible monthly; a nominal rate of discount convertible monthly; an effective annual rate of discount. (b) How large is the spread between the smallest and the largest reading? (c) What should the saver ask before comparing this plan with another? Round amounts to cents and rates to four decimals in percent.

Solution

(a) Effective 6%: \(10{,}000\times1.06^4=12{,}624.77\). Convertible monthly: \(1+i=1.005^{12}\), \(i=6.1678\%\), value \(10{,}000\times1.005^{48}=12{,}704.89\). Discount convertible monthly: \(1+i=0.995^{-12}\), \(i=6.1996\%\), value \(10{,}000\times0.995^{-48}=12{,}720.15\). Effective rate of discount: \(1+i=1/0.94\), \(i=6.3830\%\), value \(10{,}000\times0.94^{-4}=12{,}808.21\).

(b) The spread is \(12{,}808.21-12{,}624.77=183.44\) dollars, 1.45% of the smallest value. The order of the four readings is the one that Exercise 4 proves for a fixed quoted number: a rate of interest grows least when it is effective (\(m=1\)), a rate of discount grows most when it is effective, and every reading as a rate of discount grows more than every reading as a rate of interest.

(c) The saver should ask for the frequency and for whether interest is credited in arrears or deducted in advance, or, better, for the effective annual rate, and compare plans by effective annual rates. A rate with no stated frequency is ambiguous, and no formula of this chapter resolves the ambiguity.

Exercise 4 (counterexample and proof). A student claims: "At the same quoted number, more frequent conversion always costs the borrower more." (a) Test the claim with the quoted number 9.5% as a nominal rate of interest and as a nominal rate of discount, at \(m=1,2,4,12\). (b) Prove that for a fixed number \(x\) with \(0\lt x\lt1\), \((1+x/m)^m\) increases with \(m\) and \((1-x/m)^{-m}\) decreases with \(m\). (c) State the corrected claim.

Solution

(a) Effective rates, in percent: as a rate of interest, 9.5000 (\(m=1\)), 9.7256 (2), 9.8438 (4), 9.9248 (12); as a rate of discount, 10.4972 (1), 10.2224 (2), 10.0920 (4), 10.0075 (12). The claim holds for rates of interest and fails for rates of discount, where a more frequent conversion costs less. Offer D of the running case, \(d^{(4)}=9.5\%\), costs less than a quote of 9.5% as an effective rate of discount.

(b) Put \(y=1/m\). The logarithm of \((1+x/m)^m\) is \(\ln(1+xy)/y\), the chord slope of \(g(y)=\ln(1+xy)\), which has \(g(0)=0\) and \(g''(y)=-x^2/(1+xy)^2\lt0\). By the chord lemma of the section on the frequency of compounding, the slope strictly decreases in \(y\), so it strictly increases in \(m\). The logarithm of \((1-x/m)^{-m}\) is \(-\ln(1-xy)/y\), the chord slope of \(h(y)=-\ln(1-xy)\), defined for \(xy\lt1\), which holds for \(y\le1\) because \(x\lt1\); \(h(0)=0\) and \(h''(y)=x^2/(1-xy)^2\gt0\). The slope strictly increases in \(y\), so it strictly decreases in \(m\). Both chord slopes tend to \(x\) as \(y\to0\), so both effective rates tend to \(e^x-1=9.9659\%\) at \(x=9.5\%\): from below for interest, from above for discount. Because each sequence is strictly monotone, every effective rate of a discount quote lies above \(e^x-1\) and every effective rate of an interest quote lies below it.

(c) At the same quoted number, more frequent conversion costs more for a nominal rate of interest and less for a nominal rate of discount, and every quote of a rate of discount costs more than every quote of the same number as a rate of interest.

Exercise 5 (design and judgment). (a) A lender quotes \(i^{(4)}=8\%\). Find the equivalent \(d^{(12)}\) directly from the master equation (2.11), without computing \(i\), and check it through \(i\). (b) Deposit 1 is quoted at \(i^{(2)}=5\%\); deposit 2 is quoted at a nominal rate of discount of 4.95% convertible monthly, \(d^{(12)}=4.95\%\). Which earns more? Restate deposit 2 as an \(i^{(2)}\) directly. Round rates to four decimals in percent.

Solution

(a) Set \((1-d^{(12)}/12)^{-12}=(1+0.08/4)^4\). Raise both sides to the power \(-1/12\): \(1-d^{(12)}/12=1.02^{-1/3}\), so \(d^{(12)}=12(1-1.02^{-1/3})=7.8950\%\). The exponent \(-4/12=-1/3\) converts four quarterly factors into one monthly discount factor. Check: \(1+i=1.02^4=1.08243216\), \(i=8.2432\%\), and (2.10) gives \(d^{(12)}=12(1-1.08243216^{-1/12})=7.8950\%\).

(b) Effective rates: deposit 1, \(1.025^2-1=5.0625\%\); deposit 2, \((1-0.0495/12)^{-12}-1=5.0853\%\). Deposit 2 earns more although its quoted number is lower. Directly from (2.11): \(1+i^{(2)}/2=(1-0.0495/12)^{-6}\), so the second deposit's equivalent \(i^{(2)}=2[(1-0.004125)^{-6}-1]=5.0223\%\gt5\%\). On the common scale \(i^{(2)}\), the comparison is between 5.0223% and 5%.

Exercise 6 (trap: a market discount rate). A 26-week Treasury bill of face value 1,000,000 dollars is quoted at a discount rate of 4.8% (an invented quote). (a) Price it by (2.16), with 182 days to maturity. (b) A student reads the quote as \(d^{(2)}=4.8\%\) and prices the bill as \(1{,}000{,}000(1-0.048/2)\). Find the student's price and the error. (c) Find the effective annual rate of interest of the bill under each price, with a 365-day year and compound interest (2.1). Round prices to cents and rates to four decimals in percent.

Solution

(a) \(1{,}000{,}000\,(1-0.048\times182/360)=975{,}733.33\) dollars.

(b) \(1{,}000{,}000\times(1-0.024)=976{,}000.00\). The student overprices the bill by 266.67 dollars, because 182 days are \(182/360=0.50556\) of a 360-day year, not half of it.

(c) Correct price: \(1+i=(1{,}000{,}000/975{,}733.33)^{365/182}\), \(i=5.0501\%\). Student's price: \(1+i=(1/0.976)^{365/182}\), \(i=4.9925\%\). The quoted 4.8% is neither rate. The choice of a 365-day year for the effective rate is a convention chosen for this exercise; another day count gives another effective rate.
